# CAT VARC / RC research notes (2020–2025)

This is a practical synthesis of publicly available slot analyses and question-paper reproductions, not an official IIM topic taxonomy. CAT does not publish a canonical list of RC topics or the underlying full source passages. Topic names and difficulty ratings can differ across coaching analyses, so treat the examples below as reported themes rather than a precise frequency census.

## What the six-year pattern says

- CAT 2020 was the outlier in size: 26 VARC questions, commonly reported as 18 RC + 8 VA. By 2021 the format was 24 questions (16 RC + 8 VA), and that 4-passage / 16-RC-question, 8-VA-question structure persisted through 2025.
- The subject matter is deliberately broad: philosophy and intellectual history, social science/economics, culture/language/literature, ecology/biology, technology/AI, and public policy/history all recur. A topic list is not a prediction list.
- The more durable preparation target is passage structure: claim, qualification, evidence/example, counterposition, and implication. Reported analyses increasingly emphasize abstract/conceptual passages and close answer choices from 2022 onward, but this is a qualitative coaching-source trend, not an official CAT statistic.
- RC selection and accuracy matter: one passage can be concrete and evidence-led while another is abstract, requiring careful reconstruction of distinctions. Broad familiarity helps with fluency, but outside knowledge must not substitute for what the passage establishes.

## Subtopics worth building practice around

1. **Philosophy and epistemology:** definition versus example; competing definitions; what counts as knowledge/evidence; hidden assumptions; distinctions between a theory and a stronger claim.
2. **Economics, policy and institutions:** correlation versus causation; incentives and unintended consequences; individual case versus population claim; distributional effects; limits of an intervention.
3. **Science, ecology and biology:** mechanism versus observed association; necessary versus sufficient conditions; evidence strength; adapting a model beyond its original scope; uncertainty and competing explanations.
4. **History and anthropology:** chronology versus causality; evidence versus interpretation; actor attribution; present-day categories projected onto the past; local practice versus general rule.
5. **Language, literature and aesthetics:** descriptive versus normative claims; author versus narrator; medium versus content; changes in meaning across context; example mistaken for the thesis.
6. **Technology and AI:** capability versus understanding; tool versus agent; optimization versus purpose; correlation in evaluation versus real-world performance; benefits weighed against externalities.

## CAT-style question and distractor design

For six-question source-based practice sets, use a deliberately mixed blueprint rather than six factual checks:

1. central claim / best summary (full-scope answer, not a paragraph-level detail);
2. supported inference (requires combining two claims without adding a premise);
3. role of an example, analogy, or piece of evidence in the argument;
4. author’s stance, qualification, or distinction between two positions;
5. “if true” strengthen/weaken or counterfactual application;
6. boundary test: which conclusion is *not* licensed, or which paraphrase subtly changes scope.

Construct distractors around recognizable reasoning errors: a true detail that is not the answer; a correct point with the wrong scope; reversing cause and effect; turning a possibility into a certainty; confusing an objection with the author’s own view; using an example as the thesis; omitting a qualification; importing outside knowledge; or selecting an answer that addresses a nearby but different question. Keep all four options plausible, parallel in specificity, and grounded in the article. Solutions should explain why the key follows and identify the precise trap in each distractor.

## Article-library direction

The existing library already has permission-designated Aeon essays spanning identity/narrative, Stoicism, religion, intelligence/optimization, AI, neuroplasticity, biological memory, consciousness, history, language/power, art, and leisure. Those categories map well to CAT’s philosophy, science, history, culture, technology, and social-science mix. The library intentionally links to the publisher and does not store article passages.

For any additional Nautilus, Aeon, or Scientific American entries, first confirm the article is 3,000–6,000 words and obtain written permission or a license that covers AI-assisted question generation and public quiz distribution. The sites being readable without a paywall does not by itself grant those rights. Current publisher terms reviewed for this project:

- [Aeon Terms of Use](https://aeon.co/terms-of-use) prohibit copying/scraping/extracting content for AI/ML, text/data mining or analysis, and AI-generated/synthesized use absent permission.
- [Nautilus Terms of Service](https://nautil.us/terms-of-service) reserve ownership of site text and prohibit copying/reproducing/distributing it except with explicit authorization or as allowed by law. They instruct users to link to copyrighted stories rather than repost them.
- [Scientific American Terms of Use](https://www.scientificamerican.com/page/terms-of-use/) describe a revocable license for personal, non-commercial use. Scientific American provides a [permissions/licensing contact path](https://www.scientificamerican.com/contact-us/) for reuse.

This note does not make a legal determination. To generate quizzes for newly selected articles, provide the publisher’s written authorization/license for that workflow or a source text you own/control and are authorized to use.
